Let's break it down · An interactive notebook on Vaswani et al. (2017)

Attention, by hand

This is a hands-on walkthrough of the Transformer from Attention Is All You Need, the 2017 paper by researchers at Google Brain, Google Research and the University of Toronto. You type a sentence, and every number the model computes is worked out in front of you, step by step, with the matrix shapes written next to it. Then you do the same sums yourself.

The model has two halves. We go through them one at a time, starting with the encoder, and then put the trained model to work translating a new sentence.

Who this is for

For people who like to learn architectures from scratch

This notebook is for anyone who enjoys pulling a model apart and rebuilding it with a pencil. You don't need to have trained a neural network before. You only need to be comfortable multiplying and adding numbers, and to be curious about why each step is there.

The goal is simple. Once you've walked through the interactive steps and done the homework on each one, you should carry a clear mental image of how the Transformer works, from a sentence going in to the vectors coming out. With that picture in place, newer variants stop looking like new architectures. They read as small, understandable edits to this one.

Building this tutorial is how I learn. I hope it nudges you to do the same: take the pieces, imagine the forward pass your own way, and make an even more vivid version of it.

Is this how ChatGPT works?

Not exactly. Most of today's large language models keep only the decoder half, and they change details such as how positions are encoded and where normalization happens. But the building blocks you'll see here (attention, multiple heads, residual connections, layer norm and the feed-forward network) are still at the heart of every one of them. Once these click, any newer architecture reads like a set of small edits to this one.

Part 1 · Encoder

One set of sizes, start to finish

Every size in the Transformer is a design choice the authors fixed before training. The paper's base model uses the red values (§3, Table 3). A 512-wide matrix is impossible to read, so this notebook runs the whole forward pass with the blue sizes. They keep the same proportions: d_k = d_model / h and d_ff = 4 × d_model.

Every shape note shows both: this run and paper. The homework asks you to work out the paper's shapes by hand.

First time here? Keep the recommended sizes so you can focus on the forward pass. Once it clicks, change them and watch every shape follow.

Chapter 1

From words to vectors

1

Write a sentence

Everything that follows is computed from whatever you type here, live in your browser. Try a sentence with a repeated word, or try “dog bites man” and then “man bites dog”.

Up to 8 words, so every matrix stays readable.

2

Tokenize

The model can't read letters, so the sentence is cut into tokens and each token is replaced by its number in a fixed vocabulary.

The paper used byte-pair encoding with a shared vocabulary of about 37,000 tokens for English–German, and a 32,000 word-piece vocabulary for English–French §5.1. Byte-pair encoding often splits rare words into pieces. To keep things readable we split on whole words here, and the IDs are illustrative.

Out: a list of n integers. ({n},) in this run and ({n},) in the paper. Nothing has a width yet: these are just numbers that point into a table.

sentence → tokens → vocabulary IDs

3

Look up the embeddings

The embedding layer is a learned table with one row per vocabulary entry and d_model columns. In the paper that's 37,000 × 512, about 18.9 million numbers. In this run each row has {d} numbers. Each token ID simply picks out its row §3.4.

Stack those rows and you get the matrix E: one row per token, d_model columns.

NoticeThe same word always gets the same row, wherever it sits in the sentence. A repeated word produces two identical rows. Keep that in mind for chapter 2.
IDs ({n},) → E: {n} × {d} in this run · {n} × 512 in the paper.

each ID selects one row of the embedding table

4

Scale by √d_model

The paper multiplies every embedding by √d_model before going further §3.4. In this run that's √{d} ≈ {sqd}. In the paper it's √512 ≈ 22.63.

The paper doesn't say why. A common reading is that it brings the learned embeddings, which start out small, up to a size comparable with the positional encodings added next, so word meaning isn't drowned out by position.

Every entry is multiplied by the same number, so the shape doesn't change: {n} × {d} → {n} × {d} · paper {n} × 512.
Chapter 2

Where is each word?

5

Attention is blind to order

Attention compares every token with every other token all at once. There is no recurrence and no convolution, so nothing in the maths knows which word came first §3.5.

The card on the right proves it with your own sentence. It runs one full encoder layer on the embeddings without positional information, once in your word order and once shuffled. Each word comes out with exactly the same vector.

Why it mattersWithout position, “dog bites man” and “man bites dog” look identical to the model. Position has to be written into the vectors themselves.
6

How could you write position into a vector?

Before looking at the paper's answer, try designing one yourself. A good position code should be:

Bounded: small numbers, so it doesn't drown out the word's meaning.
Unique: every position gets a different code.
Consistent: position 3 means the same thing in a short sentence and a long one.
Smooth: nearby positions get similar codes, and “3 steps apart” looks the same everywhere.
Unlimited: it works for sentences longer than any seen in training.

Flip through the candidates on the card. The binary counter is the key clue. Its lowest bit flips every step and higher bits flip more and more slowly, like clock hands at different speeds. Sine and cosine waves are the smooth version of that counter, and they tick every box.

Where the idea went nextThe paper's insight was that a fixed, smooth formula could do the job of a learned table. Later work kept building on it. Relative position representations (Shaw et al., 2018) encode distances between tokens directly. Rotary position embedding, or RoPE (Su et al., 2021), takes the same sine/cosine rotation you'll see in step 7 and applies it to the queries and keys, and many of today's large language models use it.
7

Positional encoding: a clock for every position

Each position gets its own fixed vector, the same width as an embedding. Its numbers come in pairs. Each pair is a sine and a cosine of the same angle, and each pair turns at its own speed §3.5:

PE[pos, 2i] = sin(pos / 100002i/d_model)
PE[pos, 2i+1] = cos(pos / 100002i/d_model)

Think of each pair as a clock hand, with sine and cosine as the coordinates of its tip. The first hand spins fast and tells neighbours apart. Later hands crawl and tell apart positions that are far away. No two positions put all hands in the same place, so every position gets a unique fingerprint.

The word never enters the formula. Only the position and the column do.

Why a sine and a cosine? One number alone is ambiguous: sin(1) and sin(2.14) are both 0.84. The pair pins the hand down. It also makes “k steps later” a fixed rotation of every pair, a simple multiplication the model can learn. The card shows this rotation with real numbers.

8

Build the PE matrix

Fill in the formula for every row (position) and column. In this run d_model = {d}, so there are {pairs} pairs with divisors {divs}.

At the paper's size there are 256 pairs, with wavelengths growing geometrically from 2π to 10000·2π positions §3.5. The canvas below the matrix shows the real 512-wide version for 50 positions.

Sinusoids or learned?The authors also tried learned position vectors, one trained row per position, and got nearly identical results (Table 3, row E). They kept sinusoids because they “may allow the model to extrapolate to sequence lengths longer than the ones encountered during training.” A learned table simply has no row for a position it never saw, while the formula works for any position.
Sinusoidal (chosen)25.8BLEU, dev set · no parameters · any length
Learned25.7BLEU, dev set · one row per position · fixed max length
PE: {n} × {d} · paper {n} × 512. Exactly the embedding's shape, so the two can be added.
9

Add them together

The positional encoding is added, number by number, to the scaled embeddings. This is why PE has exactly d_model columns: addition needs identical shapes.

Watch the repeated words: rows that were identical in E are now different. The result X is the input to encoder layer 1. The paper also applies dropout (10%) to this sum during training §5.4.

{n} × {d} + {n} × {d} = {n} × {d} · paper {n} × 512. Addition keeps the shape, and every later step is designed to give it back.
Chapter 3 · inside encoder layer 1

Multi-head self-attention

10

Project X into queries, keys and values

Each head owns three learned weight matrices, WQ, WK and WV, each d_model × d_k. Multiplying X by each gives every token three new vectors §3.2.2:

Query: what this token is looking for.
Key: what this token can be matched on.
Value: what this token passes along if it's picked.

The weights are the same for every token and every sentence. Only X changes, which is why each token gets its own Q, K and V. In a trained model the weights are learned. Here they're random.

Matrix multiplication rule: (a × b) · (b × c) = a × c. The inner sizes must match, and then they disappear.
X {n} × {d} · W {d} × {dk} = {n} × {dk}
paper: {n} × 512 · 512 × 64 = {n} × 64
One row per token is kept. The width shrinks to d_k = d_model / h.
11

Score every pair of tokens

Each query is compared with each key using a dot product: multiply matching entries and add them up. A large score means “this key matches what I'm looking for”.

Doing it for all pairs at once is a single multiplication, Q · Kᵀ. Transposing K turns its tokens into columns.

Q {n} × {dk} · Kᵀ {dk} × {n} = {n} × {n} · paper {n} × {n}.
The width d_k cancels out and a token-by-token table appears. Row = who is looking, column = who is being looked at. Its size depends only on sentence length.
12

Scale, then softmax each row

Divide every score by √d_k: √{dk} ≈ {sqdk} in this run, √64 = 8 in the paper. Then apply softmax to each row: take ex of every entry and divide by the row total. Each row becomes a set of positive weights adding up to 1 §3.2.1.

Why divide by √d_k?If the entries of q and k are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, their dot product has variance d_k (§3.2.1, footnote 4). With d_k = 64, scores get large, softmax puts nearly all its weight on one token, and the gradients become tiny. Dividing by √d_k brings the variance back to 1. Tick the box on the card to see the difference.
The shape doesn't change: {n} × {n} · paper {n} × {n}. Each row is now a recipe: “take this share of each token”.
13

Blend the values

Multiply the attention weights by V. Each output row is a weighted average of all the value vectors, mixed in the proportions that token's row asked for.

This is the moment information actually moves between words. In a trained model, “sat” might take most of its new vector from “cat”, so it now carries information about who is sitting.

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKᵀ / √d_k) · V Eq. 1

A {n} × {n} · V {n} × {dk} = {n} × {dk} · paper {n} × 64. The inner token dimension cancels, and we're back to one vector per token.
14

Join the heads and mix them with WO

All heads run in parallel, each with its own weights, so each can learn to look for a different kind of relationship. Their outputs are placed side by side, which is called concatenation.

Concatenation alone doesn't combine anything: each head's numbers sit in their own block of columns. A final learned matrix WO mixes them, so every output number blends every head. It also maps the result back into X's space, ready to be added to X.

{h} heads × {n} × {dk} → concat {n} × {d} · WO {d} × {d} = {n} × {d}
paper: 8 × 64 = 512, so concat gives back exactly d_model, then · WO 512 × 512. Splitting d_model across heads keeps the total cost about the same as one full-width head §3.2.2.
15

Add & Norm

Add (residual connection): the layer's input X is added back to the attention output. Attention produces an update, not a replacement, so each token keeps its own identity and position. It also gives gradients a direct path through all the layers, which makes deep stacks trainable.

Norm (layer normalization): each token's row is rescaled to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 across its numbers, then multiplied by a learned scale γ and shifted by a learned β. This keeps values on a steady scale from layer to layer §3.1.

output = LayerNorm(x + Sublayer(x))

The residual is the reason every sublayer must output {n} × {d} (paper {n} × 512). You can only add matrices of the same shape.
Chapter 4 · still inside layer 1

Feed-forward network

16

Expand, then ReLU

Attention moved information between tokens. The feed-forward network now processes each token on its own with the same small two-layer network §3.3, Eq. 2.

FFN(x) = max(0, x·W1 + b1)·W2 + b2

First it expands each token from d_model to d_ff numbers: {d} → {dff} in this run, 512 → 2048 in the paper. Then ReLU replaces every negative number with 0. Without ReLU, two multiplications in a row would collapse into one, and the network could only learn straight-line relationships.

X1 {n} × {d} · W1 {d} × {dff} = {n} × {dff} · paper W1 512 × 2048 → {n} × 2048.
d_ff = 4 × d_model is a design choice. Table 3 shows 1024 → 25.4 BLEU, 2048 → 25.8, 4096 → 26.2.
17

Compress back to d_model

The second multiplication brings each token back to d_model numbers so it can be added to the residual. Row “cat” of the result depends only on row “cat” of the input. No information crosses between tokens here.

This is where most of a layer's parameters live: about 2.1 million of the 3.15 million in each paper-sized layer.

H {n} × {dff} · W2 {dff} × {d} = {n} × {d} · paper W2 2048 × 512 → {n} × 512. The width goes up and back down; the number of rows never changes.
18

Add & Norm, again

The same pattern as after attention: add the FFN's input back in, then normalize each row. The result is the output of encoder layer 1.

LayerNorm(X1 + F): {n} × {d} in, {n} × {d} out (paper {n} × 512). A full layer gives back exactly the shape it was given, which is what lets layers stack.
Chapter 5

Layers on layers

19

Repeat N = {N} times

Layer 1's output becomes layer 2's input, and so on up to the last layer §3.1. The paper uses N = 6. Every layer has the same steps but its own weights: WQ, WK, WV, WO, W1, W2, γ and β.

Each pass lets every token gather a little more context from the others. Press play to send your sentence up the stack. Every layer on the card is computed for real, with its own random weights.

Every arrow carries {n} × {d} (paper {n} × 512). The shape never changes between layers.
20

The encoder's output

Out comes Z: one vector per token, each now describing that word in the context of the whole sentence. Compare it with step 3, where every word had a fixed vector that knew nothing about its neighbours.

Z isn't turned back into words. It's handed to every layer of the decoder, which turns it into keys and values for encoder–decoder attention. That's Part 2.

Part 2 · Decoder

A machine that predicts the next word

The encoder turned your sentence into Z, one context-aware vector per word. The decoder's job is narrower than it looks. At every position it answers a single question: given the English sentence and the German words so far, what is the next German word?

Everything new in this part follows from that job. The decoder reads the translation shifted right. Its self-attention is masked, so no position can peek at the word it has to predict. Its cross-attention takes questions from the German side and answers from Z. And it ends with a linear layer and softmax that turn vectors back into words.

This part reuses your Part 1 sentence and sizes: d_model {d}, h {h}, d_k {dk}, d_ff {dff}, N {N}. Change them in Part 1 and everything here follows.

Chapter 1

What goes in

1

Write the translation

The decoder works with two inputs. One is Z, the encoder's output for your sentence from Part 1. The other is the German sentence it should produce. During training the correct translation is known, so we hand it to the model. Type it here or pick an example.

Up to 7 words. The weights are random, so any words work. A real translation just makes the pictures easier to read.

Where Z comes fromZ is computed live from your Part 1 sentence and sizes. Change the sentence there, and every card here is recomputed.
In: Z {n} × {d} from the encoder · paper {n} × 512, plus a list of German words.

2

Shift it one place to the right

The decoder is trained to predict the next word. So it reads the translation moved one place to the right, behind a start token ⟨s⟩, and at every position it must produce the word that comes next. The last position must produce the end token ⟨/s⟩, which means “done” §3.1.

Read the card column by column. Click a position to see what it has seen and what it must predict.

Why a start token?Position 0 has to predict the first word, so its input can't be that word. ⟨s⟩ stands for “nothing yet”. The paper describes this as the output embeddings being “offset by one position”.
{tw} words → decoder input of m = {m} tokens: ({m},) · paper ({m},). The targets have the same length, one per position.
3

Embed and add positions

Exactly the encoder's steps 3 to 9. Each token picks its row of the embedding table, the rows are multiplied by √d_model, and the positional encoding is added §3.4, §3.5.

The paper uses one vocabulary shared by English and German, and one embedding table for both stacks (and, as you'll see in chapter 5, for the output layer). Position k also gets the same PE vector in both stacks.

Y0: {m} × {d} · paper {m} × 512. The decoder has its own length m = {m}. It doesn't have to match the source length n = {n}.
Chapter 2 · inside decoder layer 1

Masked self-attention

4

Queries, keys and values, all from the German side

The first sublayer is self-attention, like the encoder's: Q, K and V are all computed from the same matrix, Y0. So the German words look at each other here, not at the English ones.

The decoder has its own weight matrices, separate from the encoder's. Each layer of each stack learns its own.

Y0 {m} × {d} · W {d} × {dk} = {m} × {dk} · paper {m} × 512 · 512 × 64 = {m} × 64.
5

Score, then mask the future

The scores are computed as in the encoder, S = Q · Kᵀ ÷ √d_k: every German position against every other one. Then comes the one new idea. Every entry above the diagonal, where the key's position is later than the query's, is replaced with −∞ before the softmax §3.2.3.

So row “{t1}” can still see ⟨s⟩ and itself, but nothing after it.

Why mask?Position k is trained to predict the word at input position k + 1. Without the mask that word sits right there in the input, and the model would simply learn to copy it. It would look perfect in training and fail completely when translating, because then the next word doesn't exist yet.
Q {m} × {dk} · Kᵀ {dk} × {m} = {m} × {m} · paper {m} × {m}. {masked} of the {mm} entries are masked. The mask doesn't change the shape.
6

Softmax gives the future zero weight

Softmax works row by row, as before. Because e−∞ = 0, a masked entry gets exactly zero weight. The allowed entries share the whole weight, so every row still adds up to 1.

The first row can only see itself, so its single weight is 1. Each row below sees one more word.

Tick the box on the card to remove the mask and see where the model would look if it were allowed to cheat.

The shape stays {m} × {m} · paper {m} × {m}, with zeros above the diagonal.
7

Blend the values and join the heads

From here it is the encoder's steps 13 and 14. Each row of A · V is a weighted average of value vectors, but only of positions at or before that row, because the later ones have weight 0.

The heads are then concatenated and mixed by their own WO, which brings the width back to d_model.

A {m} × {m} · V {m} × {dk} = {m} × {dk} per head → concat · WO = {m} × {d} · paper {m} × 512.
8

Add & Norm

The same residual connection and layer normalization as in the encoder. Y0 is added back to the attention output, and each row is rescaled to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 §3.1.

The result, Y1, holds each German position mixed with the German words before it. It is the input to the next sublayer.

{m} × {d} in, {m} × {d} out · paper {m} × 512.
Chapter 3 · still inside layer 1

Cross-attention: reading the encoder

9

Q from the decoder, K and V from the encoder

This sublayer is the bridge between the two halves. The paper calls it encoder–decoder attention §3.2.3. The maths is the same as every attention so far. What changes is where the inputs come from:

Queries come from the decoder, Y1: one per German position. Each asks “which English words do I need for my next word?”
Keys and values come from the encoder output Z: one per English word.

One Z for every layerEvery decoder layer reads the same Z, the output of the last encoder layer. Each decoder layer has its own WK and WV, so each one can read Z in its own way.
Q: Y1 {m} × {d} · WQ = {m} × {dk}
K, V: Z {n} × {d} · W = {n} × {dk}
paper: {m} × 64 and {n} × 64. Different numbers of rows, and that's fine.
10

A score for every German–English pair

Each German query is compared with each English key by a dot product, all at once with Q · Kᵀ. For the first time the score table is not square: rows are German positions, columns are English words.

Q {m} × {dk} · Kᵀ {dk} × {n} = {m} × {n} · paper {m} × {n}.
Rows = number of queries, columns = number of keys.
11

Softmax over the English words

Scale by √d_k and softmax each row, exactly like the encoder. There is no mask here. The whole English sentence is known before the decoder starts, so every position may look at every English word.

Each row is a soft alignment: how much each English word matters for this German position's next word. In a trained model the row that has to produce “katze” would lean on “cat”. With random weights the pattern is arbitrary.

{m} × {n} · paper {m} × {n}. Each row adds up to 1 across the English words.
12

Pull in the English information

Multiplying by V mixes the English words' value vectors into each German position, in the proportions its row asked for. This is the moment information crosses from the encoder into the decoder.

Then the heads are concatenated and mixed by WO as usual.

A {m} × {n} · V {n} × {dk} = {m} × {dk}. The English dimension cancels, and we're back to one row per German position. Concat · WO → {m} × {d} · paper {m} × 512.
13

Add & Norm

Add Y1 back and normalize each row. Y2 now carries two kinds of context: the German words so far, from masked self-attention, and the English sentence, from cross-attention.

{m} × {d} in, {m} × {d} out · paper {m} × 512.
Chapter 4

Feed-forward, and the stack

14

Feed-forward network

The same two-layer network as the encoder's, with its own weights §3.3. Each position is expanded to d_ff numbers, passed through ReLU, then compressed back to d_model. Each position is processed on its own, so nothing moves between positions here.

Y2 {m} × {d} → H {m} × {dff} → F {m} × {d} · paper {m} × 2048 in the middle.
15

Add & Norm, a third time

The decoder layer has three sublayers, so it has three Add & Norms. This last one gives the output of decoder layer 1. Like every layer, it hands back exactly the shape it was given.

{m} × {d} in, {m} × {d} out · paper {m} × 512.
16

Repeat N = {N} times

Layer 1's output goes into layer 2, and so on §3.1. The paper uses 6 decoder layers, each with its own weights for all three sublayers.

Notice the second input on the card. Every decoder layer's cross-attention reads the same Z, the encoder's final output. Press play to send your translation up the stack.

Every arrow up the stack carries {m} × {d}, and every arrow from the encoder carries {n} × {d} (paper {m} × 512 and {n} × 512).
Chapter 5

From vectors back to words

17

A score for every word in the vocabulary

The last decoder layer gives one d_model-wide vector per position. A linear layer turns each vector into one score, called a logit, per vocabulary word. The paper ties this layer to the embedding table: its weight matrix is the embedding table, transposed §3.4.

So each logit is a dot product between the decoder's vector and one word's embedding. It asks “how much does what I want to say look like this word?”

The paper scores all ~37,000 vocabulary entries. To keep the card readable we score only {V} of them: the words of your translation and ⟨/s⟩.

{m} × {d} · Wembᵀ {d} × {V} = {m} × {V} · paper {m} × 512 · 512 × 37000 = {m} × 37000.
18

Softmax: probabilities for the next word

Softmax each row and you get a probability for every word at every position. During training the loss is minus the log of the probability given to the correct next word, averaged over the positions. This is cross-entropy; the paper also smooths the targets a little (label smoothing 0.1) §5.4.

Why are the guesses wrong?The weights are random, so the model hasn't learned anything yet. Training nudges every weight in both stacks so that the correct word's probability goes up and the loss goes down.
{m} × {V} · paper {m} × 37000. Each row adds up to 1.
Chapter 6

Training vs. translating

19

Training: every position at once

During training the correct translation is known, so the whole shifted sentence goes in at once and all {m} predictions come out of a single pass. The input is always the true previous words, even where the model would have guessed differently. This is called teacher forcing.

The mask is what makes this honest. {m} predictions are computed side by side, yet each one has only seen the words to its left, exactly as it would when translating.

One pass: {m} predictions and {m} loss terms. The encoder also runs once and its Z is shared by all of them.
20

Translating: one word at a time

When translating new text there is no German sentence to feed in. The encoder runs once. Then the decoder starts from ⟨s⟩ alone, predicts a word, appends it to its own input and runs again, until it predicts ⟨/s⟩.

Step through it on the card. Only the last row's prediction is used at each step. And because of the mask, every earlier row comes out exactly as it did on the step before, so real implementations keep those rows' keys and values instead of recomputing them (a “KV cache”).

Greedy or beam search?The card always takes the most likely word. The paper instead keeps the 4 best partial translations at each step (beam search with a length penalty of α = 0.6) and stops at the source length + 50 §6.1. Part 3 slows this loop down, step by step, and builds both the KV cache and beam search.
At step t: self-attention scores t × t, cross-attention scores t × {n}, and only the last of the t rows of probabilities, 1 × {V}, is used. Z stays {n} × {d} throughout.
21

The whole decoder

One row per German position went in. Out came one probability distribution per position over the whole vocabulary. During training that gives the loss; when translating, the last row gives the next word.

The card lists every step and its shape. Notice which steps have n rows (the encoder's side) and which have m (the decoder's side), and that the only place they meet is cross-attention.

That's the model as it is trained. Part 3 puts it to work on a sentence with no translation to copy from.

Part 3 · Inference

Translating a sentence nobody has translated yet

In Part 2 the correct German sentence was handed to the decoder, and all its predictions came out of one pass. That is training. Once the model is trained, it is used on new English sentences, and there is no German to hand it. It has to write the translation itself, one word at a time, reading back every word it has already written.

This part slows that loop right down. You'll encode the sentence once, start the decoder from ⟨s⟩, follow one turn of the loop row by row, run it until it stops, and then see the two tricks every real system uses: a KV cache so old rows are never recomputed, and beam search so one bad early pick doesn't ruin the sentence.

Same model, same weights as Parts 1 and 2: your sentence ({n} tokens), d_model {d}, h {h}, N {N}. The vocabulary has {V} entries: the words of your Part 2 translation plus ⟨/s⟩. Change the sizes in Part 1 or the translation in Part 2 and everything here follows.

Chapter 1

Before the loop

1

What changes when there's no answer key

The model is exactly the one you built: same layers, same weights, same shapes. Only the way it is used changes. During training the whole correct translation goes in at once and every position is graded. During inference, also called decoding or generation, the model only has the English sentence.

So the decoder works the way you'd write a sentence by hand: write a word, read what you have so far, write the next one. The paper calls this auto-regressive: “consuming the previously generated symbols as additional input when generating the next” §3.

PlantThe mask from Part 2 is the reason this works. It trained every position to predict its next word while seeing only the words to its left, which is exactly the situation at inference time. Keep that in mind for step 11.
In: one English sentence. Out: a German sentence of a length nobody chose in advance.
2

Encode once

The English sentence is fully known, so the encoder runs one time, exactly as in Part 1, and produces Z. Nothing the decoder writes later changes it.

That has a useful consequence. Every decoder layer's cross-attention turns Z into keys and values, K = Z · WK and V = Z · WV. Those don't depend on the German words either, so they can be computed once per layer and reused at every step. With {N} layers and {h} heads that's {N} × 2 small matrices, made before the first German word exists.

Z: {n} × {d} · paper {n} × 512. Cross-attention K and V per head: {n} × {dk} · paper {n} × 64.
3

Start with ⟨s⟩

The decoder needs something to read before it has written anything. That's the job of the start token ⟨s⟩, the same token that sat in front of the shifted-right translation in Part 2. The decoder's whole input is one token long.

It goes through the usual recipe: look up its embedding, multiply by √d_model, add the positional encoding for position 0 §3.4, §3.5. At position 0 every angle is 0, so PE is just 0, 1, 0, 1, … (sin 0 = 0, cos 0 = 1).

Y0: 1 × {d} · paper 1 × 512. One row, because there is one token.
Chapter 2 · one turn of the loop

From the words so far to the next word

4

Run the decoder on everything so far

At turn t the decoder's input is t tokens: ⟨s⟩ and the t − 1 words it has written. They go through all N decoder layers exactly as in Part 2: masked self-attention among the German tokens, cross-attention into Z, feed-forward, add & norm after each.

Pick a turn on the card. The self-attention table is t × t and grows by one row and one column per step. The cross-attention table is t × {n}: it grows taller, but always has one column per English word.

NoticeAt turn 1 the only token is ⟨s⟩, so its self-attention row is a single 1.00: it can only attend to itself.
At turn {t}: decoder output {t} × {d} · paper {t} × 512; self-attention {t} × {t}; cross-attention {t} × {n}.
5

Keep only the last row

The decoder hands back one row per input token, but every row except the last is predicting a word we already have: row 0 predicts the word after ⟨s⟩, which we wrote at step 1. Only the last row looks past the end of what's been written.

So only that row goes through the final linear layer, which (as in Part 2) is the embedding table transposed §3.4. Each logit is the dot product of the last row with one word's embedding.

Last row 1 × {d} · Wembᵀ {d} × {V} = logits 1 × {V} · paper 1 × 512 · 512 × 37000 = 1 × 37000.
6

Turn the scores into probabilities

Softmax turns the row of logits into one probability per vocabulary word, adding up to 1. This is the model's whole answer to “what comes next?”: not a word, but a distribution over every word it knows.

In training this row was compared with the correct word to get the loss. Here there is no correct word, so the next step has to choose.

P: 1 × {V} · paper 1 × 37000. The row adds up to 1.
7

Choose a word

The simplest rule is greedy decoding: take the word with the highest probability. It's fast, has no settings, and always gives the same answer for the same sentence.

It isn't the only rule. You could keep several candidates alive (beam search, step 12, which is what the paper does), or roll a die weighted by the probabilities (sampling).

Where the idea went nextChat assistants usually sample instead, because the single most likely sentence tends to be bland and repetitive. A temperature divides the logits before the softmax: below 1 sharpens the distribution towards greedy, above 1 flattens it. Top-k and top-p (“nucleus”) sampling only roll among the most likely words. Translation, where one right answer matters more than variety, sticks with beam search.
1 × V probabilities in, one token out.
8

Feed it back in

The chosen word is appended to the decoder's input, and the next turn of the loop starts with one more token. That word now gets its own embedding, its own positional encoding (position t), and becomes something later words can attend to.

Compare this with training. There, position t always read the true previous word (teacher forcing). Here it reads the model's own previous pick, right or wrong. A mistake early on is read back by every later step.

Training vs. inferenceThis mismatch has a name, exposure bias: during training the model never sees its own mistakes, so it never practises recovering from them. Beam search (step 12) is one way to soften it.
Decoder input: ({t},) → ({tn},) tokens. Z stays {n} × {d}.
Chapter 3

Round and round

9

Run the loop to the end

Steps 4 to 8 are one turn. Inference is just that turn, repeated: run the decoder on the tokens so far, keep the last row, softmax, pick, append.

Play it on the card. Every turn runs the whole decoder stack again on a sequence one token longer. The English side never changes; Z and its keys and values are the same at every turn.

The weights are random stand-ins, so the words this model picks are nonsense. The loop is exactly the one a trained model runs; only the numbers inside differ. The top row on the card shows what a trained model would write for your sentence.

This run: {T} turns, decoder input from (1,) to ({T},) tokens.
10

When to stop

The loop has no fixed length. It stops when the model picks the end token ⟨/s⟩. During training, ⟨/s⟩ was the word the last position had to predict, so the model learned when a translation is finished.

A model can also fail to say ⟨/s⟩ and ramble on, so there is always a safety limit. The paper sets the maximum output length to the input length + 50 §6.1.

Stops at ⟨/s⟩, or after {n} + 50 tokens for this sentence. This card caps the toy run at 8.
Chapter 4

Faster, and better

11

The KV cache: never redo a row

Here's the pay-off of the mask. Because row r can only see rows 0 to r, appending a new token cannot change any earlier row, in any layer. Step through the loop in step 9 and you'll see rows 0 to t − 2 come out identical every time.

So recomputing them is wasted work. Real implementations keep each layer's self-attention keys and values for the tokens already processed (the KV cache) and, at every turn, push only the new token's row through the stack: one query, one new key and value appended to the cache, one row of scores against everything cached.

Where did the mask go?With a cache, the new row is the last row, and everything in the cache is in its past. There is no future to hide, so at inference time the mask has nothing left to do. It mattered during training, where all rows ran at once.
Per turn with the cache: Q 1 × {dk}, scores 1 × t, output 1 × {d}. The cache grows by one row per layer per turn: t × {dk} per head · paper t × 64.
12

Beam search: keep the best few

Greedy commits to one word per turn and never looks back. But the best sentence is not always made of the best word at every turn: a slightly less likely first word can lead to a far more likely sentence.

Beam search keeps the k best partial translations (the beam) instead of one. Each turn, every one of them is extended by every word, each candidate is scored by the sum of the log-probabilities of its words, and only the top k survive. The paper uses k = 4 §6.1; the card uses k = 2 so you can follow every candidate.

Summing log-probabilities always makes longer sentences look worse, since every extra word adds a negative number. So finished translations are compared after dividing by a length penalty, lp = ((5 + length) / 6)α, with α = 0.6 in the paper (from Wu et al., 2016).

Each turn: k beams × {V} words = {kV} candidates scored, k kept · paper 4 × 37000 = 148,000 candidates, 4 kept.
13

The whole inference, in one picture

The encoder reads the English sentence once. The decoder then writes the translation one token at a time, each turn reading Z through cross-attention and its own earlier words through self-attention, until it writes ⟨/s⟩.

The card lists every step with its shape. Compare it with Part 2's table: the same operations, but the German side now grows from 1 row to T rows, one turn at a time, and only one row per turn is new.
