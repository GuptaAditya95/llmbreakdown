BERT, by hand

This is a hands-on walkthrough of BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding, the paper by Jacob Devlin, Ming-Wei Chang, Kenton Lee and Kristina Toutanova at Google AI Language. You type two sentences, and every number BERT computes is worked out in front of you, step by step, with the matrix shapes written next to it. Then you do the same sums yourself.

If you've done Attention, by hand, you already know most of BERT. It is the Transformer's encoder with the decoder taken away, a slightly different input, and two small heads on top that give it a new job. Part 1 matches every step to the encoder you know. Part 2 is the part that's new.

Who this is for

For people who like to learn architectures from scratch

This notebook is for anyone who enjoys pulling a model apart and rebuilding it with a pencil. You don't need to have trained a neural network before. You only need to be comfortable multiplying and adding numbers, and to be curious about why each step is there.

The goal is simple. Once you've walked through the interactive steps and done the homework on each one, you should carry a clear mental image of BERT: the Transformer encoder you already know, fed two sentences at once, trained by filling in blanks and judging whether one sentence follows another. With that picture in place, the many BERT variants read as small edits to it.

Building this tutorial is how I learn. I hope it nudges you to do the same: take the pieces, imagine the forward pass your own way, and make an even more vivid version of it.

Is BERT a chatbot?

No. BERT reads; it doesn't write. Chat models such as ChatGPT keep the Transformer's decoder and predict the next word from the words on its left. BERT keeps the encoder and fills in blanks using the words on both sides. That makes it good at understanding text: classifying it, finding answers in it, turning it into vectors for search. Encoders in BERT's style still do that work today. Later models changed its training recipe (RoBERTa dropped next sentence prediction, ELECTRA replaced masking with spotting swapped tokens) but kept the picture you'll build here.

Part 1 · Encoder

One set of sizes, start to finish

The paper names BERT's sizes L (layers), H (hidden size) and A (attention heads). BERT-base uses the red values (§3). A 768-wide matrix is impossible to read, so this notebook runs the whole forward pass with the blue sizes. They keep the same proportions: d_k = H / A and d_ff = 4H.

H is the same thing the Transformer paper called d_model: the width of every token vector. Every shape note shows both sizes: this run and paper. The homework asks you to work out the paper's shapes by hand.

First time here? Keep the recommended sizes so you can focus on the forward pass. Once it clicks, change them and watch every shape follow.

Before we start

You already know this encoder

The paper is blunt about it: BERT's model is a multi-layer bidirectional Transformer encoder, and the implementation is “almost identical to the original” §3. So instead of learning a new architecture, hold the encoder from Attention, by hand in your head and look for the edits.

There are only a few. The decoder is gone. The input carries two sentences and is built from three learned embeddings. The feed-forward network uses GELU. The stack is bigger. And on top sit two small heads that give the encoder its training job, which is Part 2.

Two sentences, one sequence

1

Everything that follows is computed from what you type here, live in your browser. BERT's input can be one sentence or a pair, and during pre-training it is always a pair §3. A “sentence” can be any stretch of text, not necessarily a grammatical sentence.

Half of the pre-training pairs are real neighbours from a book or article (label IsNext), and half pair sentence A with a random sentence from elsewhere (NotNext) §3.1. Tell the notebook which kind yours is; Part 2 uses the label.

BERT cuts text into WordPiece tokens from a 30,000-piece vocabulary §3. Common words stay whole; rarer words are split into known pieces, and a piece that continues a word starts with ##. The paper's own example splits “playing” into play ##ing (Figure 2).

Then three special tokens are added. [CLS] goes in front of everything; its final vector will summarise the whole input. [SEP] closes sentence A and closes sentence B.

Our splitter is a toy: it knows a few suffixes and a few hand-made splits. The special IDs are the ones in the released vocabulary code; the other IDs are illustrative.

3

Hide 15% of the tokens

Here is BERT's central trick. A model that sees the words on both sides of every word can't be trained to predict the next word: in a deep stack, each word could indirectly “see itself” §3.1. So BERT plays a fill-in-the-blanks game instead, the masked language model.

15% of the token positions are chosen at random. Each chosen position is then replaced by [MASK] 80% of the time, by a random token 10% of the time, and left unchanged 10% of the time §3.1, A.1. Here that's {k} of the {n} positions.

Why not always [MASK]? Because [MASK] never appears once BERT is put to work, and the model must not learn that only [MASK] positions matter. It has to predict the original token at every chosen position, so it keeps a good vector for every token A.1.

5

Look up the segment embeddings

A second learned table with just two rows, EA and EB. Each token takes the row of its segment §3, Figure 2. Every token of sentence A gets the identical vector EA, and every token of B gets EB.

New in BERTThe table is tiny: 2 × 768 = 1,536 numbers in the whole model.
Table 2 × {d} · paper 2 × 768. Lookup: {n} × {d} · paper {n} × 768.
7

Look up the position embeddings

Attention is blind to order, so position has to be written into the vectors, exactly as in the Transformer. But BERT doesn't use the sine-and-cosine clocks. It uses a third learned table, one trainable row per position 0, 1, 2, … up to 511 §3, A.2.

The Transformer paper had already tried this and got nearly the same result as sinusoids (its Table 3, row E). The catch is the one it warned about: a learned table has no row for a position it never trained on. BERT's table stops at 512, and that is BERT's maximum input length. Appendix A.2 says BERT trained on 128-token inputs for 90% of the steps, then on 512 for the rest “to learn the positional embeddings”.

Table 512 × {d} · paper 512 × 768; we use rows 0 to {nm1}: {n} × {d} · paper {n} × 768.
8

Add the three together

“For a given token, its input representation is constructed by summing the corresponding token, segment, and position embeddings” §3. Number by number, the three matrices are added.

Each row now carries three facts in one vector: which piece, which sentence and where. Repeated tokens, which had identical token rows, now differ, because their positions differ.

Changed in BERTThe Transformer added two things (scaled embedding + PE). BERT adds three, all learned. Attention, by hand · step 9

9

Before the first layer, the released code normalizes each row of the sum: subtract the row's mean, divide by its standard deviation, then scale by a learned γ and shift by a learned β code. During training it also applies dropout (10%, on all layers A.2).

The paper itself stops at the sum. This step is a detail of the implementation, but it's part of every real forward pass, so we do it too.

Self-attention, both directions

10

Project X0 into queries, keys and values

Each head owns three learned weight matrices, WQ, WK and WV, each H × d_k. Multiplying X0 by each gives every token a query (what it is looking for), a key (what it can be matched on) and a value (what it passes along).

The code also adds a bias to each projection code. We leave the biases at 0, their starting value, to keep the sums short.

Same as the TransformerLine for line. Attention, by hand · step 10
X0 {n} × {d} · W {d} × {dk} = {n} × {dk}
paper: {n} × 768 · 768 × 64 = {n} × 64, with d_k = 768 / 12.
11

Score every pair, with no mask

Each query is compared with each key by a dot product, all at once as Q · Kᵀ. Nothing is hidden: every token scores every token, to its left and to its right, in its own sentence and in the other one.

That is the “bidirectional” in BERT's name. The contrast the paper draws is with OpenAI GPT, which masks the future so each token only sees its left, like the Transformer's decoder §3, footnote 4. The dashed cells on the card are sentence A looking at sentence B and back: self-attention gives BERT “bidirectional cross attention between two sentences” for free §3.2.

Same as the TransformerThe Transformer's encoder was already unmasked. BERT's choice was to pre-train an encoder, not a decoder. Attention, by hand · step 11
Q {n} × {dk} · Kᵀ {dk} × {n} = {n} × {n} · paper {n} × {n}. Row = who is looking, column = who is looked at.

Chapter 4 · still inside layer 1

Feed-forward, now with GELU

16

Expand, then GELU

Each token on its own is expanded from H to 4H numbers: {d} → {dff} here, 768 → 3072 in BERT-base §3, footnote 3. Then a nonlinearity bends the numbers so that two multiplications don't collapse into one.

The nonlinearity is the one thing inside the layer that changed. BERT uses GELU “rather than the standard relu, following OpenAI GPT” A.2. GELU(x) = x · Φ(x), where Φ is the bell curve's cumulative probability. Large positive numbers pass almost unchanged and large negative ones go to about 0, like ReLU, but near 0 the curve is smooth and small negative numbers leak through slightly.

GELU(x) ≈ 0.5 · x · (1 + tanh(0.7979 · (x + 0.044715 · x³)))

That tanh form is what the released code computes code. GELU(−1) ≈ −0.16, GELU(0) = 0, GELU(1) ≈ 0.84.

Changed in BERTReLU → GELU. The widths and the shapes are exactly the Transformer's. Attention, by hand · step 16
X1 {n} × {d} · W1 {d} × {dff} = {n} × {dff} · paper 768 × 3072 → {n} × 3072.

20

The final hidden states: C and T

Out comes one vector per token, each now describing its token in the context of both sentences. The paper names them §3, Figure 1: Ti is the final vector of token i, and C is the final vector of [CLS], position 0.

Compare with step 5, where every token had a fixed vector that knew nothing about its neighbours. If a word appears twice with two meanings (try the “bank” example), its two token rows were identical. Its two final vectors are not.

The encoder is the same machine as the Transformer's. What's different is what reads its output. There is no decoder. Instead, during pre-training, the T at the masked positions and the vector C go to two small heads. That's Part 2.

Part 2 · Heads, loss and inference

The part that's actually new

Part 1 ended with T, one vector per token, and C, the vector of [CLS]. The Transformer would now hand those to a decoder. BERT hands them to two small heads, each with one job.

The masked LM head looks only at the hidden positions and guesses the original token, scoring every piece of the vocabulary. The next sentence head looks only at C and guesses whether sentence B really followed sentence A. Each head's guess is graded with a loss, a single number that says how wrong it was, and the two losses are added into one number that training makes smaller §3.1, A.2.

Then the heads' job is done. When BERT is used, nothing is added: you either keep the masked LM head to fill in a blank, or swap both heads for a small layer trained on your task. Flip between the three pictures on the right.

This part reuses your Part 1 sentences and sizes: H {d}, A {h}, L {N}, and {k} masked position{ks}. Change them in Part 1 and everything here follows.

Chapter 1 · task #1

The masked LM head

1

Keep only the masked rows

“The final hidden vectors corresponding to the mask tokens are fed into an output softmax over the vocabulary” §3.1. Only the positions chosen in Part 1, step 3 are predicted. Every other row of T is simply not used by this head code: gather_indexes.

All three kinds of chosen position are kept: the [MASK]s, the random swaps and the ones left unchanged. The model can't tell which is which, so it has to take every position seriously A.1.

NoticeUnlike a denoising autoencoder, BERT doesn't rebuild the whole input, only these few positions §3.1. That's why it needs many training steps: each sentence gives only about 15% as many predictions as a left-to-right model would A.1, C.1.
T {n} × {d} → keep {k} row{ks}: {k} × {d} · paper {k} × 768.
2

Transform: dense, GELU, LayerNorm

The paper just says the vectors go into a softmax. The released code first passes them through one more small layer: an H × H dense layer, GELU, and a LayerNorm code: cls/predictions/transform.

You've seen all three pieces already, in Part 1's feed-forward network and Add & Norm. Here they adapt the general-purpose vectors of the last layer into “guess-a-token” vectors.

{k} × {d} · Wt {d} × {d} = {k} × {d}, then GELU and LayerNorm keep the shape · paper {k} × 768.
3

Score every piece with the embedding table

Each transformed vector is compared with every vocabulary piece by a dot product, giving one score (a logit) per piece. The weights for this comparison are the token embedding table from Part 1, step 5, used a second time and transposed code, plus one small learned bias per piece.

So each logit asks: “how much does what I think belongs here look like this piece's embedding?” Sharing the table is called weight tying. The Transformer tied its output layer to its embeddings in the same way (its §3.4).

The real head scores all 30,000 pieces. To keep the card readable we score {V}: the pieces of your two sentences plus a few common words.

{k} × {d} · Etokᵀ {d} × {V} = {k} × {V} · paper {k} × 768 · 768 × 30000 = {k} × 30000.
4

Softmax, then the MLM loss

Softmax turns each row of logits into a probability for every piece. The head is graded on one number per row: the probability it gave the original token. Its loss is minus the log of that probability, “cross entropy” §3.1. A confident right guess costs about 0; a confident wrong one costs a lot.

The MLM loss is the average over the {k} masked position{ks} A.2: “the mean masked LM likelihood”. Positions that were swapped for a random token, or left alone, are graded on their original token too.

Why are the guesses wrong?The weights are random stand-ins, so BERT hasn't learned anything yet. Pre-training adjusts every weight, in the head and in all 12 encoder layers, until the right pieces get high probability.
P: {k} × {V} · paper {k} × 30000, each row adding up to 1. The MLM loss is a single number.
Chapter 2 · task #2

The next sentence head

5

Pool the [CLS] vector

The second head only reads C, the final vector of [CLS] §3.1, Figure 1. Because [CLS] attended to every token of both sentences in every layer, its vector is a natural place to summarise the pair.

The released code first runs C through a “pooler”: one H × H dense layer and a tanh, which squeezes every number into −1 to 1 code: get_pooled_output.

CarefulThe paper warns that C “is not a meaningful sentence representation without fine-tuning” footnote 6. Pre-training only teaches it to answer this one yes-or-no question.
C 1 × {d} · Wp {d} × {d} = 1 × {d} · paper 1 × 768.
6

Two scores: IsNext or NotNext

A 2 × H weight matrix turns the pooled vector into two logits, one per label, and a softmax turns them into two probabilities code. The NSP loss is minus the log of the probability given to the true label you picked in Part 1.

The task is easy for a trained model (97–98% accuracy, footnote 5), but the paper found it helped: without NSP, question answering and inference tasks got worse §5.1, Table 5.

pooled 1 × {d} · Wnspᵀ {d} × 2 = 1 × 2 · paper 1 × 2. Two probabilities, then one number: the NSP loss.
Chapter 3

Two losses, added

7

Add the two losses into one number

Here is the sentence from the paper: “The training loss is the sum of the mean masked LM likelihood and the mean next sentence prediction likelihood” A.2. In plain words, total loss = MLM loss + NSP loss.

It's easy to picture this as the two heads' outputs being added into one answer. That's a natural first reading, and it's worth sharpening, because what gets added is smaller than that. The heads' outputs can't be added: one is a probability for every vocabulary piece at every masked position, the other is a pair of probabilities for IsNext and NotNext. They mean different things and don't even have the same shape. What gets added are their two grades, each a single number saying how wrong its head was.

Why add them at all? So one backward pass can improve both tasks at once. Both losses flow back into the same encoder and the same embedding tables, so the shared weights are pulled toward vectors that are good at filling blanks and at relating two sentences.

Only during pre-trainingThe sum is a training signal. Nothing is added when BERT is used: chapter 4 shows what happens instead.
MLM loss (one number) + NSP loss (one number) = total loss (one number). Paper and this run alike: a scalar.
Chapter 4

Using BERT: inference

8

Way 1: fill in the blank

Inference means running a trained model on new text and reading off an answer. No loss, no backward pass, no dropout, and no random masking: the 80/10/10 game was only a way to train.

To ask BERT to fill in a blank, you place [MASK] where you want a guess, run the encoder once, and read the masked LM head's softmax at that position. The NSP head and the pooler aren't used, and nothing is added. Click tokens on the card to hide them.

Compare it with the Transformer's decoder, which wrote one word, fed it back in, and ran again. BERT reads the whole input in one pass and guesses every [MASK] at the same time. The flip side: two blanks are guessed independently, neither seeing the other's guess, which is one reason BERT isn't used to write long text. Attention, by hand · decoder step 20

One pass: T {n} × {d}, then {kf} × {V} probabilities for {kf} blank{kfs} · paper {kf} × 30000.
9

Way 2: swap the heads for your task

Most of BERT's results come from fine-tuning §3.2, §4. Both pre-training heads are thrown away. One small new layer goes on top of the same encoder, and all the weights are trained a little more on labelled examples of your task: three epochs for the GLUE tasks §4.1.

Then using it is the simplest forward pass in this notebook: your input, with nothing masked, goes through the encoder once; the new layer reads C or the Ti; out comes the answer. The card computes three kinds of head on your sentences, with random stand-in weights.

Pair → label (e.g. MNLI): softmax(C Wᵀ) with W of size K × H §4.1. Answer span (SQuAD): a start vector S and an end vector E score every Ti of the paragraph §4.2. Tag every word (NER): each word's first piece gets its own label §5.3.

§4.1 writes the classifier on C directly; the released classifier reads the pooled C from step 5 code: run_classifier.py.

Pair → label: C 1 × {d} · Wᵀ {d} × 3 = 1 × 3 · paper 1 × 768 · 768 × 3. The new W has only 3 × 768 = 2,304 weights.

Pre-training: a masked pair goes in, the encoder computes T and C, the masked LM head grades itself on the hidden tokens, the next sentence head grades itself on the label, and the two grades are added into one loss that improves everything.

Using BERT: the same encoder, run once on your text. On top sits either the masked LM head (fill in a blank) or a small layer fine-tuned for your task. There is no loss and nothing is summed. The card lists every step's shape and the differences side by side.
